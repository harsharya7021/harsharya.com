The Architecture of Walls

Gated towers, sandstone forts, and the very human urge to wall everything in.

It was past 8:00 PM in Greater Noida, a time when the sprawling, dusty streets outside begin to feel distinctly inhospitable. "India lawless hai abhi bhi. Har jagah hi 8:00 baje ke baad ghar dubak jao," my friend Moksh said. Our instinct was to retreat to Jaypee Greens —our gated community. Jaypee is a premium bubble, a modern fortress where we can roam freely at 11:00 PM without a second thought.

We pay a premium for that security. We pay for the walls. And from Jaypee Greens, the leap to national defense felt inevitable. We were discussing PalantirIn comparing Palantir to the physical walls of Jaisalmer, the metaphor holds, but the mechanics differ. Palantir doesn't build weapons; it builds software (specifically platforms like Gotham and Foundry) that integrates massive, siloed datasets.

In modern warfare and intelligence, the "invading elephant" isn't an army at the gates—it is a hidden terror cell, a disrupted supply chain, or a cyber threat.

Palantir acts as a digital Jaisalmer, finding patterns in the chaos to build a predictive "wall" against unseen enemies.

The name itself is borrowed from Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings—the seeing-stones that allow the user to look across vast distances and times.

It is the ultimate watchtower.
, the shadowy American data analytics company, and whether India needed its own version. Society, we realised, has always been built on the concept of fortifying— "society bani thi fortify karne se." I thought of my recent trip to Jaisalmer. It wasn't just a fort; it was a fortified city. Entire ecosystems thrived behind those sandstone walls. If an invading army marched their elephants to the gates, the defenders wouldn't negotiate; they dropped massive stone boulders directly onto the elephants' skulls. Brutal, effective, and fundamentally human.

Today, we don't drop boulders; we drop predictive algorithms.

The Interview Is a Deception Game

We build walls to protect what is ours. It is an instinct that permeates every facet of our lives, even our careers. We talked about his recent job search, he astutely pointed out the inherent absurdity of the modern interview process. It is a high-stakes game of poker, a mutual performance. "Mere ko to lagta hai bhai deception game hai. Samne wala tere ko deceive kar raha hai, tu usko deceive kar raha hai."

The tech industry might screen for raw coding skills, but at a certain level, they are screening for bearability. They want to know: Is this person going to be a nightmare to work with? Can I sit next to him for ten hours a day?

It is less about what you know and more about whether you fit safely inside the cultural walls of the company.

Grade the Delta, Not the Herd

As we dug into our idlis and chole bhature, the conversation shifted from surviving the present to preparing the future. I brought up my research on education systems in Tier-2 cities like Jamshedpur. With AI seamlessly writing code in five minutes, the old middle-class dream of rote-learning your way into a secure software job is collapsing. The shadows of market failure loom large. So, what do we teach? If machines are taking over the mechanical, education must pivot to the profoundly human. We need the Head, the Heart, and the Hand. We need to teach children emotional regulation—therapy, exercise, the sheer joy of a "Zero Period" where they can just be. But capitalism is ruthless. It demands specialisation. It reduces a human being to a tiny cog monitoring the voltage of a single sensor, completely alienated from the delight of the end product.

Our current education system exacerbates this by relying on relative grading—pitting one student against another, breeding the very in-group/out-group tribalism that later manifests as casteism and religious divide.

We proposed a different metric: The Delta. Don't measure a student against the class; measure them against their own starting point. "Delta karna chahiye unko ki delta kya hai har ek bache ka. Aaya kahan se tha? End kahan pe kiya." If we graded based on personal growth, on the mistakes made and learned from, the bitter competitiveness might just dissolve into genuine curiosity.

12,000 Years of Wall-Building

Curiosity naturally dragged us backward in time. We zoomed out 12,000 years to the Agricultural Revolution, the moment we stopped wandering and started building our first crude walls. We debated the Harappan civilizationDuring our chat, the timelines and narratives of ancient India got a bit compressed.

The 12,000-year mark refers to the Neolithic Revolution (the dawn of agriculture and animal domestication globally).

The Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilisation peaked much later, around 2500 BCE.

When we discussed the "Aryan Invasion" from Iran bringing casteism, it's vital to update this with modern historiography. The "Invasion" theory has been widely debunked in favor of a "Migration" theory.

Indo-Aryan speaking pastoralists from the Eurasian Steppes didn't arrive in a single, violent pillaging horde. They migrated in waves over centuries, bringing early Vedic culture and Sanskrit, which gradually intermingled with the indigenous populations (who had already begun migrating eastward due to the drying of the Saraswati river system). Caste (Varna), while rooted in these ancient texts, evolved into its rigid, oppressive form over millennia of social and political engineering, not overnight., marveling at their fired-brick houses and flushing toilets. But we also recognized our tendency to romanticize the past.

"Are unhone colony bana di jaise baccha nahi hota. hum chokte hain are wah kya hoshiyar bacha hai," I argued. The Harappans were like a brilliant toddler. We praise a toddler for using a restroom independently because it is advanced for their developmental stage. But the Harappan's were still primitive compared to the staggering, exponential complexity of the modern world.

Before these grand, unified civilizations, religion was fiercely local. "Sab ke apne apne gods the," we noted. Every tribe, every river, every forest had its deity. Polytheism was an attempt to explain the terrifying randomness of nature. But humans inherently crave simplicity; we want to reduce the messy, grey areas of life into objective, binary markers. Thus came the Abrahamic push toward Monotheism—the consolidation of divine power into a single symbol. As Carl Jung pointed out in Man and His Symbols, we desperately need symbols to convey the overwhelmingly subjective experience of existence.

Even the story of Jesus, we reasoned, was rooted in a socio-political struggle. It was a movement seeking reform within Second Temple Judaism, later transformed into a global religion by the very human, arguably selfish, need to create a unified identity against the Roman Empire.

The Animal Behind the Walls

Selfishness. We kept circling back to it. It is the engine of human survival. "Insaan jo cheez hai na, janwar hai," my friend sighed. If you remove the walls, if you remove the incentives, the raw, unfiltered nature of humanity is often terrifying.

We sought refuge in the words of the philosophers who understood this darkness best. Arthur Schopenhauer, who famously viewed human desire not as a path to happiness, but as a relentless, unquenchable thirst that leads only to suffering.

He was bitter, yes, but he diagnosed the human condition with terrifying accuracy.

And then there was Nikola Tesla. We shared a tragic, perhaps slightly romanticized, anecdote about his final days. After a lifetime of dedicating his staggering genius to humanity—gifting us the alternating current system and wireless concepts far ahead of his time—he died largely marginalised and impoverished. Legend says he lamented his life's work, stating he wished he had simply brought his mother a glass of water when she asked, rather than serving a humanity that repaid him only with humiliation and isolation.While the specific "glass of water" quote is a poetic, apocryphal distillation of his sorrow, Tesla's later life was indeed marked by profound alienation.

After his funding was pulled for projects like the Wardenclyffe Tower, he spiraled into bankruptcy.

He spent his final years feeding pigeons in New York hotel rooms, ignored by the very scientific and capitalist establishments he helped build. His story is the ultimate cautionary tale of what happens when selfless genius collides with a world driven by profit and self-interest.

To push the frontier, to truly innovate beyond the walls of society, is to invite the label of insanity. But as we sat there in the car, the harsh realities of the world pressing against the glass, we reached a comforting conclusion: "Pagalpan hi asli sanity hai." Madness is the true sanity. The ones who mindlessly follow the herd, who never question the architecture of the walls around them, are the ones living the illusion.

It was getting late. The philosophical heavy-lifting had exhausted us. "Main to time pass karna hai," I finally said, adjusting my neck brace. "Main to ghar chala jaunga, JP pe chala jaunga." We decided to head back to the fortress. The world outside those walls is chaotic, selfish, and deeply flawed. But occasionally, if you step back and observe it without illusions, it makes for a hell of a conversation.
